How to Play

Two half-sisters, Effia and Esi, are born to the same mother, Maame, in 18th-century Ghana. Their families are split apart for seven generations: one line marked by fire, the other by water. Follow both bloodlines through four rooms and recover the four pieces of Maame's black stone.

  1. Gather around one screen. Three to six players. Talk everything through together.
  2. Read the story intro at the start of each room out loud.
  3. Solve the puzzle. Every answer comes from Homegoing and what we studied in class.
  4. Check your answer. A correct answer recovers a stone fragment and unlocks the next room.
  5. Rooms get harder as you go. Room one is a warm-up. The last room brings the whole book together.
  6. Stuck? Each room has three hints, each more specific than the last.
  7. Escape by restoring the whole stone. The timer tracks how long your group takes.

    The Castle, Solved

    Effia walks the halls above the ocean, wearing the stone the woman who raised her pressed into her hand. Below her, Esi waits in the dark, and when the soldiers march her to the ship, her mother's stone stays behind in the dirt.

    Same mother. Same castle. Two completely different futures.

    why this matters

    Cape Coast Castle is a real place in present-day Ghana. British officials lived and worked on its upper floors while captive Africans were held in the dungeons below. From there they were forced onto ships through what is now called the Door of No Return.

    Gyasi opens the novel by splitting two sisters across those two floors. The slave trade did not only take people from Africa. It divided families, and it ran on deals between European traders and African leaders, including the Fante village Effia grows up in.

    The Family Tree, Rebuilt

    Seven generations on each side. On Effia's side, the black stone passes from hand to hand. On Esi's side, the loss of hers is passed down instead, along with the story of a mother left behind.

    Two families. Seven generations. One mother.

    why this matters

    Each generation lives through a different chapter of history on its side of the Atlantic:

    • Ness is enslaved on a plantation in Alabama.
    • Kojo lives free in Baltimore when the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 passes, putting even free Black people at risk of capture.
    • H is sold into the coal mines through convict leasing.
    • Willie moves north to Harlem during the Great Migration.
    • On Effia's side, Akua grows up in a missionary school, and Yaw becomes a history teacher.

    Gyasi gives each person only one chapter before moving on, so readers feel the gaps between generations that slavery and colonialism created.

    The Mines, Solved

    The mines take years of H's life. Generations later, Marcus sets out to write about the system that stole them, and finds that the story is too big for one project. To explain H, he would have to explain everything that came before and after.

    "War may be over but it ain't ended." H's cellmate

    why this matters

    The 13th Amendment ended slavery, except "as a punishment for crime." Southern states passed Black Codes that turned everyday things, like being out of work, into crimes. Then they leased prisoners to mines, farms, and companies that worked them without pay, often to death.

    Most of those prisoners were Black. Many historians call it "slavery by another name." H's chapter shows that freedom on paper did not mean freedom in practice.

    Finale

    Homecoming

    Cape Coast, Ghana · present day

    Seven generations later, Effia's descendant Marjorie and Esi's descendant Marcus grow close, never learning that they share a bloodline. Together they travel to Ghana.

    At Cape Coast Castle, Marcus stands in the women's dungeon where Esi was held. He can't breathe. He pushes through the Door of No Return and runs out onto the beach.

    Throughout the novel, Effia's family is marked by fire and Esi's by water. To restore the stone, you'll need to remember the whole book.

    Step 1 Fire or water?

    Six moments from across seven generations. Mark each one as fire or water. Not every moment names its element, so think back to what happened.

    Step 2 Her last words

    On the beach, a fire burns right at the water's edge. Marjorie walks past the flames. Marcus walks into the waves. Out in the ocean, Marjorie lifts the black stone from her neck and places it around his.

    "Welcome home."

    1. The Castle. The slave trade split one family across two floors of the same building.
    2. The Family Tree. Each generation inherited a different chapter of history.
    3. The Mines. Slavery ended on paper, but convict leasing kept Black people in forced labor.
    4. Homecoming. The descendants face the fears their families carried and return to where it began.

    why "homegoing"

    In many Black churches, a funeral is called a homegoing: the belief that death brings a person home. For enslaved people, that home was often imagined as Africa. Gyasi's title carries both meanings, and her last chapter makes it literal.

    History does not stay in the past. It is carried from one generation to the next, like the stone.
